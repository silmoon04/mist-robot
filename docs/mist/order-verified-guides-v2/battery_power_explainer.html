15 A fuse → 16 AWG → four C018 servos. Raw 3S.

Logic branch

Raw 3S → measured UBEC → regulated 5 V for ESP32 and sensors.

Signal/common ground

ESP32 → Adapter A → servo data. Servo motor current does not pass through the ESP32 or adapter.

Why use separate leg branches?

Four C018s in one leg could theoretically draw about 10.8 A at simultaneous stall. Separate branches keep the high-current route short and let one leg fault be isolated without dismantling the whole robot.

A 4700 µF / 25 V capacitor at each leg root supplies very brief current transients and reduces local voltage dips. It goes across + and GND in parallel; reversed polarity is dangerous.

The full robot must never be designed to sit at simultaneous stall. Fuses are the last layer; motion limits, current/temperature monitoring and mechanical freedom are the first layers.

XT60 versus XT90-S

XT60 is the battery interface. XT90-S is an added disconnect/anti-spark device. Changing connector size does not increase what the battery lead or XT60 can safely deliver.

Smoke stopper

Temporary current limiting for first static power after wiring changes. Remove/bypass it before movement because its voltage drop can reset servos and ESP32.

UBEC

Essential voltage separation: the C018s use raw 3S, while the ESP32 module itself operates around 3.3 V and the DevKit is normally fed from a regulated 5 V input.

What each safety layer catches—and what it cannot

Protection is layered because a connector, fuse, capacitor or software check only covers certain failures
FAULTDEFENCELIMIT

Battery/trunk wire shorts

Defence: 30 A main fuse close to the pack.

It cannot correct reversed polarity or make undersized wire safe.

One leg cable shorts or servo jams

Defence: that leg's 15 A fuse plus firmware current/temperature/position limits.

A fuse is not a precision servo protector; it primarily protects the branch wiring.

New harness was wired incorrectly

Defence: continuity/polarity checks, then smoke stopper for static first power.

Do not move motors through the stopper; voltage sag creates false resets/dropouts.

Plug-in spark charges capacitors instantly

Defence: XT90-S pre-charge/anti-spark contact and a reachable disconnect.

It does not change voltage, increase XT60 capacity or replace the main fuse.

Servo movement pulls the rail down briefly

Defence: short/good-gauge wiring and 4700 µF capacitor at each leg root.

A capacitor cannot support sustained overload. Reversing it can cause violent failure.

Raw 3S reaches low-voltage logic

Defence: measured UBEC output, labelled 5 V rail and separate connector routing.

Never “test by plugging in.” Meter the UBEC at 4.9–5.2 V first.

Battery is overcharged or cells drift apart

Defence: B6 Neo in LiPo 3S balance mode with main and balance leads attached.

LiHV mode is wrong for the 11.1 V HOOVO packs.

Robot needs to stop immediately

Defence: accessible XT90-S/loop key or battery disconnect plus software torque-off/deadman.

A software stop does not help if the controller has crashed; a buried plug is not a useful kill path.

Why the current paths are substantial

2.7 A × 16 = 43.2 A

Feetech specifies about 2.7 A stall current per ST-3215-C018 at 12 V. Four in one leg could theoretically reach 10.8 A; all 16 could reach 43.2 A.

The project’s 15 A branch and 30 A main fuses are starting values for a robot that must avoid simultaneous stall. A repeated fuse blow means stop and diagnose binding, command limits, wiring or current—not install a larger fuse.
