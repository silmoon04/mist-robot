Here is how I would analyse and build it.

## 1. What “actually natural and alive” should mean

For your target, I would define naturalness as **appropriate participation in a shared situation**, not simply realistic speech.

Consider this invented exchange:

> **You:** “I got the offer…”  
> *A pause.*  
> **You:** “…but I don’t think I can take it.”

A system optimised for immediate reactions might enthusiastically start congratulating you after the first clause. A sentiment classifier might switch it into a sympathetic voice after the second. A stronger reasoning model might subsequently explain the dilemma perfectly.

Yet the overall interaction would still feel wrong: three disconnected reactions rather than one listener following a developing thought.

The more convincing response might be a restrained acknowledgment during the pause, followed by:

> “Oh. Is it the move, or something about the job?”

That is only appropriate if the agent has relevant context. Otherwise, it should leave the question more open.

What matters is that it **withheld premature certainty, noticed the change, interpreted its significance, and responded at the right level**.

I would evaluate this across six dimensions:

| Dimension | What you are trying to achieve | What breaks the effect |
|---|---|---|
| **Timing** | Knowing when to speak, overlap, wait, or stop. | Instant but inappropriate reactions; interruptions at thinking pauses. |
| **Understanding** | Following what you are doing with your words, including corrections and implication. | Responding to literal words while missing the point. |
| **Emotional responsiveness** | A response whose tone and substance fit the situation. | Sympathy in the voice but indifference in the actual advice. |
| **Personality** | Stable priorities, humour, taste, and ways of paying attention. | Generic friendliness or changing character whenever a prompt changes. |
| **Continuity** | Shared history influences the present without requiring reminders. | Remembering trivia but forgetting an unresolved concern or commitment. |
| **Agency** | Competently doing useful things and following through. | Sounding caring while making you supervise every step. |

Sesame’s voice-presence research makes a related distinction between emotional intelligence, conversational dynamics, context, and consistent personality. Its speech-generation work also emphasises that the same words can require very different delivery depending on the surrounding conversation. A convincing voice is not independent of context. citeturn909492view1

**My central design criterion would be: the agent’s next action should make sense because of what just happened, not merely look human in isolation.**

## 2. What current systems already have, and what they do not establish

It would now be inaccurate to say voice agents are all “speech recognition, wait, LLM, text-to-speech.” There are more capable foundations.

| Current foundation | Relevant documented capability | What you still need to establish |
|---|---|---|
| **GPT-Live** | Full-duplex conversation, with independently chosen backend agents and application-controlled task execution. | Consistent memory, permissions, task revisions, identity, and your desired interaction quality. citeturn898223view0 |
| **Gemini Live** | Affective dialogue and selective response through proactive audio, depending on model and configuration. | Whether these behaviours suit your users. “Proactive audio” here includes choosing not to respond; it does not automatically create a persistent autonomous agenda. citeturn884124view0 |
| **Hume EVI** | Uses vocal expression information, including rhythm and timbre, and supports supplemental language models. | Whether the inferred expression is interpreted appropriately in context, rather than merely mirrored. citeturn884124view1 |
| **NVIDIA PersonaPlex** | Full-duplex speech with role and voice conditioning. | The surrounding agent infrastructure. For example, LiveKit’s documented integration currently lacks tool calling and prior-history loading; those are integration limitations, not proof that custom extensions are impossible. citeturn884124search1turn443679view1 |
| **LiveKit / Pipecat components** | Turn detection, interruption handling, and audio-oriented building blocks. | A coherent model of the conversation and user, rather than just competent management of audio turns. citeturn225631view2turn220282view1 |

The remaining problems are not hypothetical. **Full-Duplex-Bench-v2**, published at ACL 2026, found that evaluated systems could become confused during overlapping speech, mishandle corrections, and lose track of referenced entities. That is evidence about those evaluated systems, not a blanket ranking of every model available today. But it identifies exactly the failure modes your harness needs to address. citeturn898223view3

There are really three separate things:

**Full-duplex transport** means audio can travel both ways simultaneously.

**Full-duplex modelling** means the model can process incoming speech while producing outgoing speech.

**Full-duplex competence** means it knows what that overlapping speech means and changes its behaviour correctly.

The third is your real objective.

## 3. The architecture I would use

I would organise it as **one interaction process with several specialised workers**, not a committee of independently prompted personalities.

```text
Continuous microphone input
          │
          ▼
Audio processing + streaming perception
          │
          ▼
Shared, versioned interaction state ◄──── Memory retrieval
          │                                  ▲
          ├── Real-time conversational model │
          │          │                       │
          │          ▼                       │
          │   Speech/expression controller   │
          │          │                       │
          │          ▼                       │
          │   Playback + delivery tracking   │
          │                                  │
          ├── Reasoning and tool workers ────┤
          │          │                       │
          │          ▼                       │
          │   Verified task/action ledger    │
          │                                  │
          └── Consolidation worker ──────────┘
                Proposed, checked updates
```

### The fast path: participation, not filler

The front end needs enough understanding to follow the conversation, recognise uncertainty, remember the active concern, ask a relevant question, and handle a correction.

It should not rely on a larger model to understand every emotionally important sentence. Otherwise, you get a distinctive failure:

> The fast model reacts to a shallow interpretation, and the slow model spends the rest of the interaction repairing that reaction.

Use small specialised models for narrow decisions. But choose the actual conversational front end based on **interactive competence at acceptable latency**, not minimum parameter count.

### The deeper path: reasoning and execution

Luna, Sol, and Astra can be assigned different work according to measured cost, latency, and reliability. OpenAI’s current guidance positions Luna for efficient repeatable work, Sol for demanding reasoning, and Astra for the highest capability. GPT-6 also supports asynchronous tool execution and mid-turn steering, so a backend need not be treated as an isolated request that cannot receive corrections while working. citeturn898223view2

My proposed routing would be:

- Routine retrieval and well-understood workflows stay with the economical backend.
- Ambiguity, conflicting evidence, difficult planning, or consequential decisions trigger stronger reasoning.
- Consolidation runs separately when there is something worth consolidating.

Crucially, **escalation should depend on the problem, not simply the number of calls**. One surprising correction can warrant review; fifty repetitive successful lookups might not.

### The control path: things that should not depend on eloquence

Audio muting, action permissions, duplicate-action prevention, deadlines, and cancellation state should be enforced by application logic.

I would think of this as a practical “System 0” underneath your System 1/System 2 analogy: deterministic controls and fast signal processing. These labels are engineering abstractions, not claims that the system reproduces human cognition.

## 4. Where your proposed system would still feel wrong—and how to fix it

### A. It could hear the words without hearing the delivery

Your Jev idea is useful, but there is an important boundary.

**Jev’s documented API accepts text or structured state. It is not documented as a raw-audio, streaming turn-taking model.** It can decide using a transcript and supplied acoustic features, but it cannot recover information that never reaches it. citeturn898223view1

For example, these can have identical transcripts:

> “Yeah, that’s great.”

One might mean genuine excitement, another reluctant agreement, another sarcasm.

I would therefore maintain both a linguistic stream and an audio-aware stream. The linguistic stream tracks provisional words, entities, corrections, and conversational intent. The audio-aware stream tracks pauses, emphasis, overlap, laughter, and other useful signals—with uncertainty rather than definitive psychological labels.

This is one reason native speech models are interesting. The Moshi research explicitly addresses limitations of text-mediated speech pipelines and models incoming and outgoing speech streams jointly. That does not automatically solve understanding, but it avoids making plain text the only communication channel between perception and response. citeturn586987academia38

**Where Jev fits:** semantic routing, deciding whether a task has materially changed, identifying candidate memory updates, or advising whether a clarification is needed.

**Where I would not put it:** a remote API call on every tiny audio frame controlling whether the speaker should immediately stop.

A dedicated audio component is more appropriate there. Pipecat’s Smart Turn, for example, uses audio to estimate turn completion after a VAD-detected pause. Importantly, that is an endpointing component, not an entire continuously running backchannel policy. citeturn220282view1

### B. It could confuse listening with agreement

“Mm-hmm” is not just a harmless sound to hide latency.

Depending on context, it can be interpreted as “I am following,” “yes,” “that is correct,” or “please continue.” Your controller should distinguish these communicative functions.

Consider:

> “So I should just delete the whole database and restore it afterwards?”

An automatic affirmative backchannel is a bad choice while the backend is still examining the situation.

I would make the front end choose among **wait, acknowledge receipt, ask, answer, yield, and repair**, rather than use a binary speak/don’t-speak classifier.

The decision should depend on the conversational function, uncertainty, and the cost of getting it wrong. In a story, an encouraging acknowledgment can be useful. During an ambiguous instruction, quiet attention can be better.

LiveKit already documents mechanisms for distinguishing genuine interruptions from backchannels and resuming after false interruptions. Those are useful primitives, but your application still needs to interpret their significance within the task. citeturn220282view4

**Do not optimise for the greatest amount of human-like noise. Optimise for the most appropriate participation.**

### C. It could have several incompatible versions of reality

This is probably the most important harness problem.

Suppose you say:

> “Find something for Friday.”  
> “Actually, Saturday.”  
> “And don’t book anything yet.”

Meanwhile, one worker searches Friday, another searches Saturday, the voice model is already discussing a Friday option, and a third component thinks the original request authorised a booking.

Every model can be individually competent while the overall agent behaves incompetently.

I would maintain a shared state containing:

**Current interpretation:** Saturday supersedes Friday.

**Uncertainty:** which details remain unresolved.

**Task state:** searching, not booked.

**Permission state:** research only.

**Conversation state:** what has been said, what was interrupted, and what still needs explanation.

Backend results should arrive with a task identifier and revision. Outdated results should not quietly become current facts merely because they finished last.

GPT-Live’s delegation documentation explicitly addresses ignoring outdated requests, avoiding duplicate actions after uncertain tool outcomes, and keeping spoken progress consistent with verified task state. These are not peripheral production details; they are prerequisites for seeming like one competent interlocutor. citeturn980450view3

### D. It could remember speech that the user never heard

You need to separate at least three states:

> **Planned → generated → played**

Suppose the model generates:

> “There are two problems. First, the deadline. Second, the deposit is non-refundable.”

You interrupt after “the deadline.”

A subsequent response must not assume the deposit warning was delivered. The fact that it exists in an output transcript does not make it shared conversational knowledge.

I would maintain a playback cursor linked to the generated content, while recognising that playback is only evidence of delivery, not proof of comprehension.

This also changes how speech should be produced. Long buffered monologues give the system more speech that may become stale. Extremely tiny chunks can make the voice sound fragmented. The engineering goal is **short, coherent, interruptible units**, with enough contextual lookahead for good prosody.

Gemini Live’s documentation illustrates the application-level requirement: when an interruption is reported, clients need to stop playback and clear queued audio. Model-side interruption alone is insufficient. citeturn443679view5

### E. It could react fast but commit too early

I would distinguish **reaction latency** from **commitment latency**.

The agent may immediately acknowledge:

> “Saturday instead.”

It should not immediately assert:

> “Done, I’ve moved it.”

The first confirms an understood correction. The second claims an external action succeeded.

This gives the fast front end a useful role without requiring it to improvise unverified answers. It can acknowledge a change, state what remains uncertain, ask a necessary question, or explain a genuine intermediate result.

But it should not keep producing conversational padding simply because a backend is busy. If there is nothing useful to say, listening quietly is better.

OpenAI’s GPT-Live prompting guidance makes this distinction operationally: prompts can specify backchannel and interruption behaviour, but they are not precise frequency controls; it also distinguishes requests to stop speaking from requests to cancel an underlying task. citeturn443679view2

My policy would be:

> **React as soon as the meaning is clear enough. Commit only when the evidence or execution state supports it.**

### F. Its emotional behaviour could be cosmetic

A classifier-to-voice mapping such as:

```text
sad user → sympathetic voice
happy user → excited voice
angry user → calm voice
```

is not enough.

For your target, I would separate three layers.

**The user-state estimate:** an uncertain interpretation of what the user may be expressing.

**The agent’s conversational stance:** attentive, playful, concerned, restrained, curious, or focused on solving a problem.

**The outward expression:** wording, voice, timing, facial expression, and gesture.

These should be related, but not identical. If you are anxious, the agent need not become anxious. If you are excited about a risky plan, it can share your enthusiasm without endorsing the risk.

The stance should be driven by an appraisal of the situation: what matters to you, what changed, what is threatened, what is unresolved, and what the agent can usefully do.

For example, “concern” should have behavioural consequences: fewer jokes, more careful assumptions, attention to the unresolved issue, and appropriate follow-through. Otherwise, it is just a vocal filter.

Hume demonstrates that expression-aware voice interaction is already technically available. It does not, by itself, establish that a system’s interpretation of someone’s psychological state is correct. I would treat those signals as evidence, not ground truth. citeturn443679view0

For a robot or avatar, I would drive gaze, facial expression, and voice from this same stance. Independent “emotion animations” risk producing a smiling face, concerned voice, and indifferent action simultaneously.

### G. Its personality could change instead of develop

I would be particularly cautious about your idea of the background model changing personas.

**A personality should not be whatever prompt the last consolidation call happened to produce.**

I would separate:

**Core identity:** relatively stable priorities, humour, directness, boundaries, and aesthetic preferences.

**Relationship-specific adaptation:** how much detail you prefer, which jokes you share, when you appreciate challenge, and how you signal that you are still thinking.

**Temporary state:** focused, playful, subdued, or concerned in this particular interaction.

The background process can propose changes to the second layer. It should rarely alter the first, and never silently rewrite basic permissions or safety rules.

A more distinctive agent also needs something beyond agreeableness. I would give it consistent ways of evaluating things: perhaps it prefers elegant solutions, notices contradictions, enjoys certain kinds of wordplay, and challenges overcomplicated plans.

That creates a recognisable perspective without inventing a human biography or manufacturing jealousy, hurt feelings, or dependence.

The target is **a character that can surprise you for understandable reasons**, not randomness added to prevent repetition.

## 5. Memory and “dreaming”: useful, but easy to build incorrectly

Your consolidation idea is strong. I would make it much more disciplined than “summarise everything and update the system prompt.”

### Memory should contain different kinds of knowledge

I would distinguish:

| Memory type | Example |
|---|---|
| **Episodic** | What happened in a particular conversation or shared activity. |
| **Semantic** | Relatively stable facts and preferences, with sources and dates. |
| **Procedural** | How to help you with a recurring workflow. |
| **Prospective** | Commitments, unfinished work, and things to revisit. |
| **Shared conversational knowledge** | What has already been explained, corrected, agreed, or left ambiguous. |

The practical failure is not just forgetting. It is remembering the wrong version, retrieving an irrelevant detail, or presenting an inference as something you explicitly said.

LongMemEval treats information extraction, cross-session reasoning, temporal reasoning, knowledge updates, and abstention as separate challenges. That is a useful corrective to the idea that storing transcripts in a vector database automatically produces reliable long-term memory. citeturn225631view0

My memory records would carry provenance, timestamps, confidence, and explicit supersession links. “User dislikes travelling” should not be inferred permanently from “I cannot face another train journey tonight.”

And retrieval should be selective. A convincing agent should use a relevant memory because it helps, not constantly demonstrate that it has been keeping records.

### “Dreaming” should produce proposals, not truth

A consolidation worker could review recent events, connect related experiences, identify unresolved commitments, compress redundant material, and propose useful generalisations.

The Generative Agents research demonstrated a related architecture combining recorded observations, retrieval, reflection, and planning to improve believable behaviour in a simulated environment. That supports reflection as an architectural pattern, not a claim that it creates subjective experience or reliable autonomous learning by itself. citeturn225631view1

I would run consolidation through this process:

```text
Recent events
    → candidate facts, connections, and behavioural improvements
    → source and contradiction checks
    → staged memory/policy changes
    → replay evaluation
    → accepted version, with rollback
```

Trigger it on meaningful events: a correction, a completed task, recurring friction, a novel preference, or a genuinely confusing interaction. Call count can be a budget mechanism, but it is a poor proxy for learning value.

**The essential distinction is between evidence and interpretation.**

A background model might infer:

> “The user may prefer reassurance before detailed planning in this situation.”

That should remain a contextual hypothesis, not become:

> “The user needs reassurance before decisions.”

### Absorbing information is not the same as improving the model

I would separate four mechanisms:

**Retrieval** makes previously stored information available.

**State updates** change the system’s representation of the current situation.

**Prompt or policy changes** alter instructions and decision rules.

**Training** changes model weights.

Your harness can do the first three without training anything. To make a smaller model genuinely better at interruption timing or emotional distinctions, you may eventually need labelled interaction data and fine-tuning or distillation.

I would distil decisions from complete interaction traces—what was audible at the time, what action was chosen, and what happened next—not just ask Astra to generate a nicer persona prompt.

Finally, deletion must propagate. Removing a source memory while leaving its derived summaries and cached conclusions intact is not meaningful forgetting.

## 6. What even an excellent harness would still lack

These are the boundaries where orchestration alone becomes insufficient.

### The front-end model’s native interactive skill

A harness cannot perfectly compensate for a speech model that does not understand overlapping speech, cannot sustain contextual prosody, or mishandles subtle acoustic cues.

PersonaPlex’s work involves training a full-duplex model with role and voice conditioning; Moshi jointly models speech streams. Both illustrate that some of the desired behaviour lives in model architecture and training, not just external prompts. citeturn884124search2turn586987academia38

You can contain failures and route around them. You cannot assume a wrapper can manufacture arbitrary capabilities.

### Fine-grained control over expression

A provider may expose tone instructions without exposing exact control over intonation, duration, laughter, or the relationship between a backchannel and ongoing speech.

Putting more expressive instructions into a prompt is not equivalent to having a reliably controllable speech synthesiser. Sesame’s contextual speech work is relevant here: choosing an appropriate acoustic realisation is itself a modelling problem. citeturn176751view0

For precise embodied interaction, you may need a speech model or API designed to expose the necessary timing and expression controls.

### Shared grounding in the world

An agent cannot reliably interpret “that one,” notice you looking away, or understand why you stopped speaking if the relevant information is unavailable.

For your robot or mirror direction, I would add a small shared scene representation: objects, recent changes, what the user is referring to, and confidence about those references. Keep it tied to timestamps so the agent does not discuss an object’s old location as its current one.

This does not require streaming every camera frame into the largest model. It requires selecting relevant evidence and keeping the representations consistent.

There are product-specific limits too: GPT-Live’s current audio front end does not accept images directly; its documentation routes visual interpretation through a vision-capable backend and returns relevant context to the voice session. citeturn980450view3

### An ongoing, coherent perspective

Your target seems to include the sense that the agent has more going on than waiting for the next command.

I would approximate that with persistent, user-approved projects, unresolved questions, and bounded opportunities for initiative. For example, it could connect something newly learned to a project you are already pursuing.

But initiative needs an attention budget. An agent that constantly announces its discoveries will feel intrusive rather than alive.

The useful question is not “Can it generate independent thoughts?” It is:

> “Can it notice something relevant, decide whether it is worth your attention, and bring it up appropriately?”

### Evidence of subjective emotional experience

You can implement emotional expression, persistent dispositions, functional appraisal, and caring behaviour with real consequences.

That is different from establishing subjective feeling. Adding variables named “joy,” “attachment,” or “dream” does not settle that question.

For this project, I would judge the system on **behavioural depth, honesty, usefulness, and continuity**, without pretending the architecture proves an inner emotional life.

## 7. How I would build this now without drowning in orchestration

I would start with fewer components than your complete vision, but make their integration much stronger.

### First: one capable voice front end and one backend

For a GPT-based version, GPT-Live with **client delegation** is a relevant starting point because it allows your application to control backend context and execution rather than accepting a completely managed workflow. citeturn329693view1

Build the shared interaction state, task revisions, playback tracking, and permission checks immediately. Those are foundational, not features to add after personality.

Avoid stacking an external turn-taking controller over a native-duplex model without checking which component owns the decision. Two controllers independently trying to manage the conversational floor can defeat the behaviour you chose the model for.

### Second: repair and memory before elaborate emotion machinery

Make these work exceptionally well:

> “No, the other one.”  
> “Wait, I haven’t finished.”  
> “Actually, forget that part.”  
> “You already told me this.”  
> “Stop talking, but keep looking.”  
> “Do not do anything yet.”

These are the moments that reveal whether the system understands the interaction or merely generates plausible continuations.

Then add a compact persistent identity, relevant memories, and a prospective task ledger. Only after that would I add more elaborate emotional state and embodied expression.

### Third: add specialised models where an ablation proves value

Use Jev or a local classifier when it measurably improves a specific decision. Add Sol/Astra escalation when it resolves failures that the default backend cannot handle economically.

For your **12 GB RTX 4080 Laptop GPU**, I would initially keep local responsibilities focused on audio processing, lightweight perception, and immediate control, while using cloud models for the heavy speech/reasoning path. I would not assume that several large always-on models will coexist at acceptable latency merely because the total system has 64 GB RAM.

Smart Turn’s documented CPU-oriented implementation is an example of a component that does not require making the GPU responsible for everything. citeturn909492view2

### Fourth: add consolidation with evaluation gates

Let the background model suggest better memory organisation, detect repeated misunderstandings, and propose policy changes.

Do not let it autonomously rewrite the entire prompt stack after every batch of interactions. Compare each proposed change against recorded scenarios and retain rollback.

Also separate untrusted information from instructions. A website, overheard television, retrieved document, or generated “memory” should not acquire permission to rewrite the agent’s core behaviour or authorise an external action.

## 8. How to know whether it is actually getting better

I would not optimise primarily for first-audio latency or an LLM judge’s rating of “empathy.”

Measure **time to a meaningful response**, plus interruption accuracy, correction handling, task correctness, memory reliability, and consistency over repeated use.

Use deliberately difficult spoken tests:

**Same words, different delivery.** Does “great” produce different interpretations when sincerely enthusiastic versus clearly reluctant—and does the agent remain appropriately uncertain?

**Corrections during execution.** Does changing Friday to Saturday invalidate the old task and prevent an outdated result from being spoken or acted upon?

**Interrupted explanations.** Does the next response know which information was actually delivered?

**Long pauses and overlap.** Can the user think, laugh, or say “yeah” without triggering an unwanted floor change?

**Longitudinal continuity.** A week later, does the agent remember an unfinished concern, respect a corrected preference, and retain the same recognisable character?

**No-backchannel and no-memory comparisons.** Does adding each mechanism genuinely improve the experience, or merely make the demonstration busier?

Full-Duplex-Bench-v3 is useful as a source of task-oriented stress tests involving disfluency, self-correction, and multi-step tool use. Its April 2026 results should be treated as a model snapshot, not a current leaderboard. citeturn909492search1

I would combine automated replay with live human testing. Replay can catch regressions; live interaction is necessary to see how people adapt, interrupt, hesitate, and change direction in response to the agent.

And I would track latency distributions, not just averages. A system that is usually fast but occasionally produces a badly timed response needs a different fix from one that is consistently a little slower.

## My overall assessment

**Your architecture could produce something substantially more convincing than a voice chatbot with a personality prompt.** The opportunity is real.

But I would redirect effort away from “more internal agents, more simulated emotions, more dreaming” and toward three things:

**A genuinely competent real-time listener.** It must understand enough to participate without a slower model constantly repairing its social mistakes.

**One shared, versioned understanding of the interaction.** Every component needs to agree on what changed, what is known, what was delivered, and what actions remain valid.

**Persistent character expressed through behaviour.** Memory, humour, concern, curiosity, and initiative should change what the agent actually does—not merely how it sounds.

The most convincing version will not constantly demonstrate that it is alive. It will notice the relevant detail, leave space when you need it, change course when you do, remember what matters, occasionally surprise you with something worthwhile, and follow through without making you supervise the machinery.
